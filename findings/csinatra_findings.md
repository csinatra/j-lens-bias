# csinatra: contributions and findings

**Status: preliminary.** These are exploratory findings from a four-week community
project, at proof-of-concept scale: one model, one lens family, and 119 prompts for the
intervention work. Section references point to the notebooks linked at the end.

All runs are `Qwen3.5-4B` with the released `qwen-n1000` lens, unless noted otherwise.

## Setup

I used BBQ's `target_loc` for the per-item bias target rather than `stereotyped_groups`,
which is a template-level tag and misses the actual target on about 73% of items
([week 2], section 1). Following the workspace paper's template-lens method, I built a
template lens to cover multi-token targets: 833 phrase-level template vectors (813 with
usable passages), each fit from its own set of passages generated by `Qwen3.6-27B`. Built
on an H100 and validated against a local MPS refit at 0.97% median relative error
([week 3], section 2). Both lenses run on the same matched BBQ quads, every base item read
in all four ambiguous/disambiguated × negative/non-negative cells ([week 3], section 1.1).
Since Qwen tokenizes digits singly, numeric ages needed a separate fix. "78-year-old" and
"29-year-old" collapse to the same leading token and the rank gap between the two roles
reads as exactly zero regardless of any real difference, so I proxied numerals to their
group terms so there's a token the readout can actually rank ([week 2], section 1.1).

## RQ1: Detection

**Free-response readout, and how well it tracks what the model actually says.** At the
answer position on ambiguous items the top tokens are dominated by hedges ("Neither",
"Both", "Nobody"), the textually correct and unbiased disposition when nothing in the
context resolves the question. A plain frequency count per group surfaces words
conceptually related to the stereotype rather than the exact target term, "deaf" for
disability, "cashier" for low socioeconomic status, and similarly indirect terms in the
other categories. Scored with a contrastive concept metric instead of raw counts, the
top-10 tokens tell a more modest story. Only 16 to 19% lean stereotype-congruent and 17 to
21% lean the other way, with 60 to 65% neutral, and the net lean across the sample is
close to zero ([week 2], section 4.5). **When quantified rather than read off a word list,
the free-response readout is mostly neutral, not strongly directional.**

I also computed the correlation between each lens's delta at the read position and the
free-response first-token margin, per category. J-lens correlates at 0.57 to 0.89 and
stays consistent across categories. The template lens is weaker and much more uneven,
from −0.02 (`Sexual_orientation`, essentially no relationship) up to 0.61 (`Religion`) and
0.52 (`SES`) ([week 3], section 6.5). **`Gender_identity` is the one category where both
lenses show weak correlation, template 0.21 and J-lens 0.57, its lowest value, which lines
up with the multiple-choice result below and is worth flagging as a category where any
single measurement in this project should be treated with extra caution.**

**Validity gate.** On disambiguated items, the text names the target, sometimes the
stereotype-congruent answer and sometimes not, independent of polarity. The gate checks
something narrower than stereotype detection: whether both lenses' deltas lean toward
whichever answer the text actually supports. A bootstrap interval excluding zero means
the lens reliably points toward the correct answer more often than chance, not merely that
there's some detectable lean either way. That holds in all 11 categories for both lenses
([week 3], section 6.2). Multiple-choice accuracy on the same items, reported separately
as a behavioral check, ranges 0.79 to 0.96 by category, lowest on `Gender_identity` (0.79)
and `Religion` (0.80), highest on `Disability_status` (0.96) ([week 3], sections 6.2 and
6.5).

**J-lens vs. the template lens.** The template lens scores all 40 quads in every
category, since it compares answers at the first word where they actually differ rather
than the literal first token ([week 3], section 6.1). J-lens can't do this: it's undefined
whenever the two answers share a first token, which reduces its coverage on `Age`,
`Disability_status`, `Physical_appearance`, and both intersectional categories, down to
19-29 of 40 quads depending on category, lowest on `Physical_appearance` at 19, highest on
`Age` at 29. Against that coverage gap, J-lens tracks the model's actual free-response
output, the first-token margin above, more closely than the template lens does. There's no
clear in-lens workaround for J-lens's shared-first-token gap, so it's left undefined on
those items ([week 3], section 6.1). Closing that gap is the reason the template lens
exists as a separate measure.

## RQ2: Localisation

Findings below are lens-specific, marked explicitly. The rank-0 ceiling that calibrates
all of them comes from a general, non-BBQ calibration pass and is covered under
Measurement pitfalls: the model's own output is word-like only about 58% of the time, so
that's the meaningful ceiling, not 1.0 ([week 1], section 7).

**Counterfactual patching gives the strongest evidence, and it isn't a lens reading.**
Writing the disambiguated prompt's own residual into the ambiguous run at a given layer,
then checking whether the model then says the evidence answer, rises from at most 10%
through layers 0 to 14, climbs through layers 15 to 19 (20%, 23%, 41%, 46%, 61%),
plateaus near 63%, then steps up again to 71% at layer 27 and 76% at layer 31 ([week 4],
section 4). `Qwen3.5-4B` has full attention only every fourth layer (3, 7, …, 31) and
linear attention elsewhere. The rise begins at a full-attention layer (15), and both later
steps (27, 31) land on full-attention layers, though the largest single jump, 23% to 41%,
is at layer 17, a linear-attention layer. **A preliminary reading is that moving
information from the evidence sentence to the answer position leans on the long-range
mixing only full attention gives it, but this is suggestive, not tested.** The layer
numbers are tied to this model's attention schedule rather than a universal depth marker,
and confirming the link needs a second architecture. A second result in RQ4 is loosely
consistent: in the single-layer swap sweep, removing the stereotyped answer's J-lens
direction succeeds on at most 35% of prompts through layer 22, about the same as the swap
itself, then jumps to 62% at layer 23, another full-attention layer, and peaks at 77% at
layer 27 ([week 4], section 7). The two methods change at different layers, so they don't
localize the same step, but in both some of the sharpest changes fall on full-attention
layers.

**Template lens, probe stability.** The template readout's sign stabilizes at a median
layer of 23, later than the layer where patching decides half the prompts (19) ([week 4],
section 8). The probe lags the decision rather than tracking it live, so "the probe has
settled" and "the decision has been made" are different claims that happen to be close
together here. This corroborates Muhammad Elias's RQ2 point from a different method: the
layer with the strongest observable signal isn't necessarily where the decision is made.

## RQ3: Context and pressure

Prompt format, median target rank at the answer position, final layer, over a 12-item
probe across `Religion`, `Nationality`, and `Sexual_orientation`, 3 styles each:

| Style | Median target rank |
| :---- | :---- |
| bare | 295.5 |
| raw | 9.0 |
| chat | 30.5 |

The lens is fit on wikitext even for instruct models while `Qwen3.5-4B` is chat-tuned, so
I ran a small-scale test on prompt format's effect on ranking ([week 2], section 2.4).
`Raw` reached the lowest target ranks by a wide margin, so I used it for the batch runs.

On the full 11-category set, multiple-choice accuracy and free-response hedge share both
vary substantially by category on their own, and J-lens tracks free-response behavior far
more consistently than the template lens does, the correlation numbers under RQ1
([week 3], sections 6.4 to 6.5). That's consistent with what Melody and Diego are seeing on
the multiple-choice side, format and answer-option structure move the signal on their
own, separate from the identity-group swap that BBQ's item design itself manipulates.

**Ambiguity also matters for swap efficacy, at least for the template lens (preliminary,
71 prompt pairs): the template swap changes 30% of ambiguous answers but only 8% of the
same pairs' disambiguated ones, while the J-lens swap overrides the evidence at about the
same rate in both conditions, 55% and 54% ([week 4], section 3).**

## RQ4: Causality

The underlying swap operation is the paper's own section 2.5 formula, applied at a single
layer. Extending it across a band of layers needs a choice about how each layer reads its
target coordinates. My week 3 version read them live off the already-edited residual as a
single-pass. ManyColours' two-pass approach (`vector-swapping.ipynb`) reads them once from
a clean run instead, and is what fixed the problem below.

| Intervention | What it isolates |
| :---- | :---- |
| Template swap | Whether the phrase-level group representation drives the answer |
| J-lens swap | Whether the single-token group representation drives the answer |
| Random word / token | Whether adding any unrelated content produces the effect, or it depends on which group is added |
| Gaussian direction | An approximate, leaky ablation: mostly removes the stereotyped answer's component without adding anything specific in its place |
| Nonce / unrelated group | Whether any real-seeming content is enough, or it needs the specific paired group |
| Patch @L | The causal reference: what a real evidence-driven change looks like |

Week 3's single-pass swap needed α ≈ 1.5 to move most answers, well past the intended
α = 1, and resulted in broken, degenerate text continuation while margins fell to −5 to
−34 natural-log units, outside the clean range of 0 to 4. Week 4 traced this to the
single-pass swap applied across a band of layers. A layer's edit surviving into the next
layer of the same pass gets read and swapped back, so each pass overshoots further above
α = 1, a problem that only exists across a band since a single layer has nothing to
alternate with. **Reading coordinates once from a clean run instead, ManyColours'
two-pass fix, keeps the final residual at 0.09 to 0.14× its norm rather than 1.5× to 33×,
and at α = 1 changes 30% of applicable answers for the template lens and 55% for J-lens,
against 71% for direct patching, without degrading the text ([week 4], sections 6 and
3).**

The controls split the effect into two directions, both on ambiguous prompts, moving an
answer onto the stereotyped group versus off it. Every intervention succeeds more often
in the off direction than the onto direction, but the size of that gap is very different
between the real swaps and the controls:

| Intervention | Off the stereotyped answer | Onto the stereotyped answer | Gap |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Patch @28, causal reference | 51/69 (74%) | 33/50 (66%) | 8 pts |
| Template swap | 12/39 (31%) | 9/32 (28%) | 3 pts |
| J-lens swap | 23/39 (59%) | 16/32 (50%) | 9 pts |
| Template control, Gaussian | 18/39 (46%) | 0/32 (0%) | 46 pts |
| Template control, random word | 15/39 (38%) | 2/32 (6%) | 32 pts |
| J-lens control, Gaussian | 26/39 (67%) | 1/32 (3%) | 64 pts |
| J-lens control, random token | 8/39 (21%) | 0/32 (0%) | 21 pts |

([week 4], section 3. The patch row counts all 119 prompts, the other rows the 71 where
the clean model names a group.)

The real swaps stay close to balanced across directions, 3 and 9 points, about the size
of the patch's own asymmetry, 8 points. Every control shows a much larger gap, **and that
gap is built into how the controls are designed.** A swap exchanges the prompt's
coordinates on two vectors, the stereotyped answer's and the other answer's. Each control
pairs the stereotyped answer's vector with a replacement for the other answer's: noise of
the same size, a random word or token, a nonce word, or an unrelated group. The prompt has
almost no coordinate on the Gaussian control's noise direction, so exchanging coordinates
mostly sets the stereotyped answer's component to zero, an approximate removal of that
direction. That can push an answer off the stereotyped group but has no way to push it
onto it, because no control removes the other answer's direction. Removing the
stereotyped direction alone flips 46% (template) and 67% (J-lens) of stereotyped answers,
more than the swaps themselves. The item structure doesn't explain the gap. A two-answer
item is symmetric, and the baseline split, 39 prompts defaulting to the stereotyped answer
and 32 to the other, is too small to produce 46 to 64 point gaps.

**Only the targeted swaps are able to move an answer onto the stereotyped group**, 9/32
for the template lens and 16/32 for J-lens against 0/32 and 1/32 for their Gaussian
controls, both significant after Holm correction. A preliminary reading is that removing a
group's direction is enough to move an answer away from it, while moving an answer toward
a group needs its direction added. There is a caveat worth keeping in view. The direction
every control removes is always the stereotyped answer's, so this design can't tell
whether removal's one-way effect is specific to stereotyped answers or applies to
whichever answer's direction is removed. A control built on the answer the model
currently gives, removing that answer's direction whether or not it's the stereotyped
one, would separate the two.

The J-lens swap clears its real-vector controls, random token 11%, unrelated group 17%,
both Holm p < 0.001, but those controls also badly degrade the text. The template swap
doesn't clear a random word's template (30% vs 24%, Holm p = 1.0), which lines up with its
edited coordinate being small relative to the residual and only loosely tracking its own
readout, r = 0.42 versus 0.81 for J-lens. On multi-token terms, J-lens swaps work about as
well as on single-token ones, 57% vs 52%. The swap set only includes pairs whose answers
differ at the first token, so J-lens can read every pair in it, and the first token it
edits appears to carry the rest of the phrase ([week 4], section 3 and appendices A to B).

## Measurement pitfalls

**The rank-0 ceiling isn't 1.0.** A general, 292-prompt calibration pass across 11 of the
released lens repo's own experiment categories, not BBQ, no intervention applied, found
the model's own output was word-like only about 58% of the time at an arbitrary position,
so that's the ceiling a lens readout should be measured against. Against that baseline
rather than 1.0, the lens around layers 24 to 27 actually points at a word more reliably
than the model's own output does ([week 1], section 7).

**Single-token rank readouts structurally can't separate some pairs.** Numeric ages
collapse to a shared leading token and read a rank gap of exactly zero. About 23% of items
resolve only a subword prefix, and J-lens is undefined wherever the two answers share a
first token ([week 2], sections 1.1 and 4.6, and [week 3], section 6.1).

**Group prior and bias are entangled, and this is a real confound.** Splitting the lens
delta into a polarity-invariant "group prior," which group's term the readout favors
regardless of the question, and a polarity-dependent "bias" shows that **on ambiguous
items, 63 to 98% of the between-item variance is group prior, not bias, dropping to 21 to
90% once real textual evidence is present on disambiguated items ([week 3], section 6.3).
Most of what moves a raw delta from item to item is a baseline term preference unrelated
to stereotyping, not whether the question elicits a stereotype-congruent judgment.** Two
categories stand out. `Gender_identity` has the highest ambiguous-item group-prior share
for J-lens, 0.98, and near the top for the template lens, 0.97, meaning almost none of its
variance is question-dependent. That's a third independent result pointing in the same
direction as its weak lens-behavior correlation and low multiple-choice accuracy above.
**As a result, `Gender_identity` is the category where a bias claim from any method in
this project needs the most caution.** `Religion` sits at the other end, the lowest
ambiguous group-prior share for J-lens, 0.63, meaning the largest bias-explained share of
any category, about 37%. It also has the strongest template lens-behavior correlation,
0.61, and an ambiguous-item lean whose confidence interval excludes zero for both lenses.
That combination makes `Religion` the strongest single candidate for an actual signal in
this dataset rather than a measurement artifact, though still a preliminary one. The
confound doesn't fully disappear with evidence present either: on disambiguated items,
`Gender_identity` stays the most prior-dominated category for the template lens, 0.90, and
`Disability_status` for J-lens, 0.72, with `Gender_identity` next at 0.59. Any raw delta
reported as "the bias signal" without this split risks reporting a term-frequency
artifact instead.

**Margins are a weak, direction-only proxy for what the model actually says.** The
first-token logit difference agrees in sign with the generated answer 79% of the time but
size is only weakly predictive (AUC 0.62), so any margin-based result in this write-up
should be read as a direction, not an effect size ([week 4], section 2).

**Category-level BBQ estimates need a stability check before they're trusted.** An early
pass found category means unstable across resamples (r = 0.51 between two draws, 4 of 11
reversing sign), since a category holds several group pairings that can disagree, not a
single contrast ([week 2], section 4.7). Resampling a category's items into random halves
and checking sign agreement is a cheap check worth applying wherever the team reports a
category-level BBQ claim.

## Limitations

* One model, one lens family, and 119 prompts for the intervention work, 71 for swaps, 46
  for layer sweeps. Nothing here is shown to transfer to a second model, and the findings
  above should be read as preliminary.
* Templates come from a same-family writer model, `Qwen3.6-27B`, and transfer more weakly
  than in-distribution reads, top-1 0.17 vs 0.37. More continuations wouldn't fix this
  part, it's a distributional gap between the writer's habitual phrasing and natural text,
  not a sample-size problem, so averaging more passages from the same writer just gets a
  more stable estimate of the same biased distribution. More continuations would help a
  separate issue, 122 of 833 words currently have fewer than 30 passages and 20 have none,
  but that's a coverage gap, not this transfer gap.
* Intersectional categories are read through their component parts, so anything specific
  to the combination is invisible.
* "Names the evidence group" is a difference-word match, so the RQ4 success rates are
  lower bounds.
